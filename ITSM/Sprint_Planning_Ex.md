# Sprint Planning

Agile is based on the continuous delivery of a working product, using iterations to make improvements based on feedback. To practice Agile techniques, we’ll go through a complete sprint planning iteration for a new product. The product can be anything your team feels is appropriate for the requirements of this specific exercise. It does not have to be specifically related to this course in any way.

Some ideas to help get you started:

* A new type of exercise device
* An online menu for a restaurant
* A social network for people with shared interests
* A new smart appliance for the home
* An educational children’s toy

The first goal will be to create a version of your product with minimal functionality to make it available to stakeholders for feedback. This minimum viable product (MVP) should focus only on delivering the singular most important function of the product, as well as any supplemental features necessary to support that function.

For this exercise, you and your group will brainstorm an idea for a project and plan sprints to complete the minimum viable product for your project.

## Project Selection
Using a timer, conduct a 5-minute project brainstorming session, where each member is expected to contribute at least two ideas. The Agile planning process can be used for physical products as well as software projects, so feel free to be creative. Keep in mind that larger and more complex projects have more details to decide and take longer to plan, so the ideal project for this exercise should have limited complexity and could be built in a few months or less. It’s fine if it isn’t something you can build yourself because we’re only doing high-level planning in class.

Once you’ve picked a few options, vote as a group to select your idea. Ties can be broken by your instructor.

## Create the Backlog
Identify your product's most important feature and then begin creating a backlog of prioritized tasks required to deliver this functionality. It’s okay to document any supplemental features you come across but focus on what is required to deliver the core value of your product. Anything that is not essential for the MVP should be treated with the lowest priority.

As you identify the essential components of your product, keep in mind that there may be dependencies that require you to complete tasks in a specific order. Focus on describing all the higher-level tasks involved in creating the MVP over debating specific details or product customizations.

It’s good to start planning on a flexible medium like paper, whiteboard, sticky notes, or a shared document where it is easy to add and edit things as a group. Once you’re confident in your list of MVP features, create stories for all these items in Jira. You may want to create a new epic where you can group these stories, as well as consider naming conventions that will help you identify the purpose of an isolated story.

## Planning Poker
Next, we’ll use story points to measure the complexity of your tasks, which will help with planning sprints and estimating how long the project will take. Each group member will score the items in your list based on how many points they think it is worth, where 1 point is roughly one session of work. Points should consider both the amount of time the task will take and its complexity. Complex tasks will often take more time due to additional research and planning, or problems that come up along the way.

Planning poker is a technique used to crowdsource reasonable estimates for project tasks using the knowledge of your sprint team. Using a scoring system based on the Fibonacci sequence or something similar, team members use personal domain knowledge to roughly estimate the weight of each task. In some cases, it may make sense to give more weight to members with more domain knowledge in an area, but in general, taking the average across the team will generate reasonably accurate scores without extended debate.

You may want to download a planning poker app from your phone’s app store or use an online tool like Hatjitsu (https://hatjitsu.toolforge.org/). Group members should announce their scores simultaneously to avoid biasing each other's opinions. Use the average score for any tasks where the weight isn’t obvious or where there is a prolonged debate. As you score the items in your list, be sure to update the stories you’ve created in JIRA with the associated number of story points.

## Sprint Planning
First, you’ll need to determine how long each of your sprints should be. One- or two-week sprints are a good rule of thumb, but it's fine to adjust based on the life cycle of your product. A relatively simple project might have sprints lasting hours or days, while sprints for larger projects may take a full month. You’ll also want to strike a balance between planning enough work to remain productive and reducing the meeting overhead of additional sprint meetings.

Organize the tasks for your project in priority order. The most important tasks should be at the top of the backlog, and tasks with multiple steps should have prerequisite tasks listed before their dependencies.

Estimate the sprint capacity of your team based on the formula:

Days in Sprint * Daily Points per Member * Members on Team = Total Sprint Capacity

Assign stories for the most important tasks in your backlog to the first sprint, up to your total sprint capacity. It’s a good idea to leave a margin of error up to 25% for bugs and unexpected problems. Try to assign your team members a balanced number of story points for the sprint.

## Analysis
Answer the following questions:

Approximately how many story points can the team complete during a sprint?  
How many story points are assigned to each team member in a sprint?  
How many sprints will it take to complete your MVP?  
How many team members would be needed to complete the MVP in a 2-sprint timeframe?
